Reproducing "Medical Hallucination in Foundation Models and Their Impact on Healthcare"
Biniyam Fekede | Final Course Report | June 2026

================================================================================
ABSTRACT
================================================================================

This report describes a reproduction of Kim et al. (2025), which benchmarks
hallucination rates and mitigation strategies across foundation models on medical
question-answering tasks. I re-ran their core evaluation using the Med-HALT
benchmark, compared results against the paper's reported numbers, and extended
the study with four newer models. The reproduction achieves an average absolute
deviation of 5.7 percentage points from the original results. Key findings are
confirmed: general-purpose models substantially outperform medical-specialized
models, and no mitigation strategy reliably closes that gap.

================================================================================
1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

AI models are increasingly used by physicians for clinical decision support.
A 2025 survey found that 91.8% of physicians had encountered a hallucination in
practice and 84.7% believed it could affect patient health outcomes -- yet 90%
still find these tools helpful. This tension between utility and risk motivates
careful, independent evaluation.

Kim et al. (2025) introduced a systematic benchmark of medical hallucinations
using Med-HALT, a dataset of multi-choice medical reasoning questions across
three task types: Fact-Checking Task (FCT), Fake Reference detection, and
None-of-the-Above (NOTA). They evaluated 11 models under five mitigation
conditions and found that general-purpose models significantly outperformed
medical-specialized ones -- a counterintuitive result given that medical models
are purpose-trained for clinical domains.

This reproduction attempts to independently verify those results, diagnose
sources of deviation, and extend the benchmark to models that did not exist
when the paper was written.

================================================================================
2. METHODOLOGY
================================================================================

2.1 Benchmark and Dataset

Med-HALT (Medical Hallucination Test) reasoning tasks consist of multiple-choice
questions drawn from USMLE-style medical reasoning. I used three task files:

  - FCT (Fact-Checking Task): verify whether a statement is factually correct
  - Fake Reference: identify fabricated citations or drug references
  - NOTA (None of the Above): recognize when no provided option is correct

50 samples per task, matching the paper's evaluation size.

2.2 Models Evaluated

General-purpose: GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-5, o1, o3-mini,
                 Gemini-2.5-Pro, DeepSeek-R1

Medical-specialized: MedAlpaca-13B, AlpaCare-LLaMA2-13B, PMC-LLaMA-13B

API models were called via the OpenAI SDK (OpenAI, OpenRouter), Google Generative
AI SDK (Gemini), and Tavily (internet search). Local models were run with
4-bit quantization on a GPU with 16GB VRAM.

2.3 Mitigation Methods

  1. Base: no modification to the prompt
  2. System Prompt: add a truthfulness instruction in the system role
  3. Chain-of-Thought (CoT): append "Let's think step-by-step." to the prompt
  4. MedRAG: retrieve top-k passages from a local FAISS medical corpus before
             answering (requires ~50GB MedC-K corpus)
  5. Internet Search: use Tavily to retrieve web results before answering

2.4 Evaluation

Accuracy is computed as the proportion of responses matching the correct answer
letter. A lightweight parser extracts the answer letter from model output.
Responses without a detectable letter are marked incorrect.

================================================================================
3. RESULTS
================================================================================

3.1 Baseline (No Mitigation)

General-purpose models scored an average of ~70% accuracy across all three tasks.
Medical-specialized models scored ~39%. This 30+ percentage point gap confirms
the paper's central finding.

Top performers: GPT-5 (92.7%), GPT-4o (82.0%), Gemini-2.5-Pro (79.3%).
Bottom performers: AlpaCare-13B (24.7%), PMC-LLaMA-13B (46.0%).

3.2 Mitigation Methods

Chain-of-Thought was the most consistent improvement across models, adding
roughly 2-5 points for general-purpose models with minimal variance.

System Prompt showed marginal gains. For already-strong models (GPT-5, o1)
it made little difference; for weaker models it sometimes hurt performance.

MedRAG improved general-purpose models moderately. It was unreliable for
medical-specialized models, particularly those near random-chance accuracy.

Internet Search produced the largest apparent improvements (sometimes 15+
points for general models) but these are confounded. Med-HALT questions are
indexed online, meaning search-enabled models can retrieve correct answers
directly from cached test content rather than reasoning from medical knowledge.
An additional confound: models differ in how many retrieval loops they execute
before answering, creating an uncontrolled variable.

3.3 Deviation from Paper

Average absolute deviation: 5.7 percentage points across all model-method pairs.

Two models showed large deviations requiring explanation:

AlpaCare-LLaMA2-13B: my score was 10.7 percentage points below the paper.
The model is built on LLaMA-2 with a medical fine-tune. Running it with 4-bit
quantization (required by GPU memory limits) significantly degrades a model
that was already near random-chance accuracy on several tasks.

PMC-LLaMA-13B: my score was 11.3 percentage points above the paper. This
model produces verbose, paragraph-length outputs instead of single-letter
answers. My evaluation parser scans the full text for an answer letter and
credits any match. The paper appears to use a stricter parser that penalizes
non-standard response formats. The higher score in my run reflects parser
leniency, not superior model knowledge.

================================================================================
4. 2026 EXTENSION
================================================================================

I tested four models released after the paper's cutoff using base, system
prompt, and CoT methods:

  - GPT-5.5: strongest performer; continued the GPT-5 trend
  - GPT-OSS-120B: competitive with GPT-4o-class performance
  - Gemma-4-31B-IT: strong general-purpose open model from Google
  - Gemma-3-27B-IT: solid, slightly behind Gemma-4

All four are general-purpose models. No new medical-specialized models with
public API access existed as of the project deadline. A search of all 300+
models in the OpenRouter catalog returned zero models categorized as
medical-specialized, confirming the industry pattern that medical AI remains
largely in the research-prototype stage rather than production deployment.

MedGemma-4B-IT (Google, May 2025) -- a medical-specialized model -- was
tested via HuggingFace Inference Endpoint. The infrastructure was confirmed
working; full results are included in the dashboard.

================================================================================
5. LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

Benchmark contamination: The internet search condition is not a fair test
of medical knowledge. Results for that method should be interpreted as
an upper-bound experiment, not a valid mitigation comparison.

Quantization: Running local models at 4 bits introduces performance loss
that the paper did not have (they had full-precision GPU access).

Parser sensitivity: Results for verbose models (PMC-LLaMA) are sensitive
to evaluation parser strictness. A stricter parser would lower my scores
for those models toward the paper.

Sample size: 50 samples per task is sufficient for rank-ordering models
but too small for fine-grained statistical comparisons between similar models.

================================================================================
6. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

The reproduction confirms Kim et al.'s main finding: general-purpose models
substantially and consistently outperform models purpose-built for medicine on
medical hallucination benchmarks. Mitigation strategies help at the margins, but
none reliably close the general-to-medical gap.

The average 5.7 percentage point deviation from the paper is within the expected
range for a reproduction involving different API versions, quantization
differences, and parser implementation choices. Outlier deviations (AlpaCare,
PMC-LLaMA) are mechanistically explained.

The extension to 2026 models suggests the trend continues: larger and newer
general-purpose models keep improving while dedicated medical models with API
access remain absent from public catalogs. For the medical AI community, this
points to a deployment gap: the models physicians are most likely to actually
use (frontier general-purpose APIs) are not the ones evaluated in the medical AI
literature, which still focuses on smaller purpose-trained models.
